Burhanpur district, in the Nimar region of southern Madhya Pradesh, sits in some of the state's most fertile banana-growing territory. The Tapti River and the Satpura hills frame it on two sides, and bananas grow here in enough volume to make the district a significant producer. What the district has not historically produced in equal measure is processed banana products that travel — value-added goods that convert the surplus into something more durable than a commodity price at the mandi.

Khushboo Patil, 24, grew up watching that gap from inside it. Her family farms bananas in Borsar village. A ₹70 lakh debt accumulated over years of difficult crop cycles. She left after school, completed an MBA, and built a career in the cities — including a job with Reliance Retail. In January 2026, she came back to Burhanpur.

## ₹8–10 Lakh In. Ten People Employed. Six States Reached.

Patil registered "Yuvraj and Anita Patil Enterprises" — naming the company after her parents — and set up a small processing unit in Borsar village that converts bananas from the family's own orchard into chips. The initial investment was **₹8–10 lakh**, part of it accessed through a government MSME scheme. There was no outside investor, no co-founder, and no established customer base. She built the production process, designed the packaging, and began reaching buyers herself.

The results, in less than a year of operation, are concrete. The venture now employs **ten people** from the local area. Orders have come in from Madhya Pradesh, Jharkhand, Karnataka, Gujarat, Pune, and Indore — a six-state distribution footprint for a unit that is still, by any measure, in its early phase.

The arithmetic behind the business is straightforward: bananas sold raw from Burhanpur fetch a commodity price subject to harvest volatility and trader margins. Turned into chips, packaged and shipped, the same fruit generates substantially more value per kilogram and travels further without spoiling. Patil understood this and acted on it — from her home village rather than from a food-tech hub in Bengaluru.

She credits Prime Minister Narendra Modi's Atmanirbhar Bharat framework for the confidence to bet on local production, and the government scheme she accessed gave her early working capital. But the decision to return, to name the business after the parents who took the losses, and to hire locally rather than contract out — those came from her.

Tier-3 districts in Madhya Pradesh have agricultural surpluses that often leave the state unprocessed. Patil's venture is small and early, but it is a working example of what changes when someone with the right skills chooses to close that gap from inside the district rather than from outside it. The chips are already crossing state borders. The harder work — scaling it — comes next.
