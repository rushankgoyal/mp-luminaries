The recognition came thirty-five years after the work. On 23 October 2026, retired IAS officer Rajeev Sharma received the Jal Prahari Samman-2026 at a ceremony in Teen Murti, New Delhi—a national honour for water conservation—for an initiative he launched in 1991, when he was posted as a district officer in Damoh, Madhya Pradesh.

The story of what Sharma did in Damoh is simple and specific. Faced with a drying Rajnagar pond, he did not wait for a budget line or a government scheme. He organized local residents to contribute *shram-daan*—voluntary community labour—to deepen the pond and restore its capacity to hold water. No large contracts, no outside equipment. A district officer, a community, and a few weeks of coordinated work. The pond has been functioning as a water source in Damoh ever since.

## One Pond, Thirty-Five Years On

The Jal Prahari Samman-2026 recognized **31 water conservationists** from across India, with the ceremony hosted at Teen Murti in Delhi. **Three awardees came from Madhya Pradesh**, with Sharma among them. The award recognizes individuals whose community-focused water conservation work has had lasting, demonstrable impact—not just project completion, but outcomes that persist.

Sharma's Rajnagar pond effort belongs firmly in that category. Water conservation through community participation is a recurring challenge across Madhya Pradesh, where much of the landscape—the Malwa plateau, the Vindhya hills, the Bundelkhand fringe—faces seasonal water stress. The approaches that have proved most durable are not the largest-budget ones but those that make communities the stewards of the infrastructure. Sharma's 1991 initiative embedded exactly that kind of ownership.

What makes the recognition notable is the gap it bridges. Most administrative awards follow soon after the work. This one arrived after three and a half decades, during which the pond Sharma helped restore continued to serve its purpose quietly, without ceremony. That durability is its own argument.

**Madhya Pradesh has seen its share** of ambitious water projects—canals, dams, river-linking proposals—but the Jal Prahari Samman points to a different register: the unglamorous, ground-level conservation work that district officers and local communities have sustained together, often without credit. Sharma's recognition is a reminder that some of the most consequential administrative acts are small in scale and long in impact.

For Damoh, the Rajnagar pond remains. For the country, Rajeev Sharma's name is now on a national list of water guardians—35 years after he earned the place.
