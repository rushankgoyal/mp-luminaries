On 16 October 2026, *Prahaar: The Untold Story of Ujjwal Nikam* opens in cinemas across India — a biopic directed by Avinash Arun Dhaware and produced by Dinesh Vijan under the Maddock Films banner. Rajkummar Rao plays Ujjwal Nikam, the senior advocate who spent more than two decades as Maharashtra's most sought-after Special Public Prosecutor on terrorism cases. The film centres on the prosecution of Ajmal Kasab, the lone gunman captured alive after the November 2008 attacks in Mumbai.

Nikam, today a member of the Rajya Sabha representing the Bharatiya Janata Party from Maharashtra, began building his reputation in the city's sessions courts before two cases gave him national recognition. The first was the prosecution of the accused in the 1993 Bombay serial bombings — thirteen coordinated explosions on 12 March 1993 that killed over 250 people across the city. The trial took more than a decade. Nikam argued methodically through forensic evidence, delivery chains for the explosives, and witness testimony before the convictions were handed down. Yakub Abdul Razak Memon, found guilty of criminal conspiracy and transporting arms and explosives to India, was hanged at Nagpur Central Jail in July 2015 after the Supreme Court rejected his final plea.

## The Kasab Trial

The prosecution of Ajmal Kasab was of an entirely different scale. Kasab was captured after a gunfight in Mumbai during the coordinated attacks of November 26–29, 2008, in which 166 people were killed at the Taj Mahal Palace hotel, CST railway station, Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj Hospital, and other locations. He was the only attacker taken alive.

Nikam led the prosecution through a closely watched trial that concluded in May 2010 with Kasab's conviction on charges including murder, waging war against India, and conspiracy. The Bombay High Court confirmed the death sentence in February 2011. The Supreme Court upheld it in August 2012. Kasab was hanged at Yerwada Central Prison in Pune on 21 November 2012 — a fact the authorities revealed only after the execution was complete.

The film, according to promotional material released in advance of its opening, focuses on the craft and pressure of that prosecution: the courtroom strategy required to try a case observed by the entire nation, the evidentiary record built to survive multiple rounds of appeal, and the human dimensions of the man behind the brief. Wamiqa Gabbi, Sikander Kher and Jaideep Ahlawat appear in pivotal supporting roles.

## A Different Kind of Courtroom Hero

India's legal cinema has long favoured the defence — the embattled advocate who takes on the state, the crusader who frees the wrongly accused. *Prahaar* inverts that convention. Its subject is a prosecutor: the person responsible for assembling a case the court cannot dismiss, for presenting evidence with enough clarity and rigour to stand through appeal after appeal.

That methodical quality — not oratory but discipline, not theatre but paperwork — is what Nikam's career represents. The two cases he prosecuted most visibly are textbook exercises in building a record that holds: verifiable facts, documentary chains, no avoidable gaps. In an era when terrorism trials can founder on procedural challenges, that discipline mattered.

By bringing this story to a mass audience, *Prahaar* offers something the legal profession rarely gets: public recognition of the unglamorous craft of prosecution. For India, which has invested heavily in its anti-terrorism legal infrastructure since 2008, it is a reminder of what that infrastructure depends on — the person who walks into court with the files and makes the case.
