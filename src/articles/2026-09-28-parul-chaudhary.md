Parul Chaudhary crossed the steeplechase finish line on September 27 in a time that no Indian woman had ever run: **9:08.67**. It broke her own national record of 9:12.46 — and put her among just a handful of Asian women ever to go under 9:10. A day later she returned to the track at the 2026 Asian Games and added a second bronze, this one in the 5000m, finishing in **15:14.61**.

Two medals, two days, one national record. It was the kind of performance that defines a career.

"It feels really good," she said after the 5000m. "Last time in Hangzhou, I also took two medals, but this time the colour has changed." The reference was instructive: at the 2023 Hangzhou Asian Games, Chaudhary had taken gold in the 5000m and silver in the steeplechase. The step down to bronze this time was not a retreat — the field in 2026 was deeper and the competition sharper — but the national record she set in the steeplechase means she left these Games as a faster, more complete athlete than she arrived.

Chaudhary, 31, grew up in Iklauta village in Meerut, Uttar Pradesh, and came to competitive distance running later than most of her peers. That she has now established herself as the best female steeplechaser India has produced, and one of its finest 5000m runners, reflects the consistency she has built since her breakthrough at the 2022 Commonwealth Games.

## A National Record Built to Last

The steeplechase is among the more technically demanding events in track athletics — **2,800 metres** on a standard women's 3000m course, with 28 barriers and seven water jumps that punish even small errors in stride pattern and take-off. Chaudhary's time of 9:08.67 in Tokyo was not just a personal best; it moved India's steeplechase record to a level that will be difficult to challenge for years.

To put the figure in context: the Asian Games qualifying standard for Paris 2024 in the women's 3000m steeplechase was 9:23. Chaudhary ran nearly 15 seconds faster than that benchmark in a bronze-medal finish, in what was effectively a mid-season competitive outing.

Her 5000m bronze the following day was completed with less drama but equal substance. A time of 15:14 over five kilometres, at 31, signals not a winding down but a runner who has learned how to compete across events and pace herself through a major games.

For Indian athletics, Chaudhary's double provides something concrete to build on heading toward the next Olympic cycle. Distance running has historically been one of the harder medals for India to pursue at elite level; her results at back-to-back Asian Games suggest that the gap to the continental podium has genuinely closed.
