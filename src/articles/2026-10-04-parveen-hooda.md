There is a particular kind of pressure that comes with standing two steps below where you once stood. At the 2023 Asian Games in Hangzhou, Parveen Hooda had come home with a bronze — good enough for most, not good enough for her. Three years later in Aichi-Nagoya, she answered that question with a gold medal and a split-decision victory that the Indian camp described as "a comeback for the ages."

On October 2, 2026, Hooda stepped into the ring against Chen Nien-chin of Chinese Taipei for the women's 65 kg final. Chen is not a gentle opponent — she had won the weight class at Hangzhou and came in as the defending champion. The bout was close enough that three of the five judges split their cards, but when the scores were read out, three judges had Hooda ahead, two had Chen. The gold went to India. Hooda's arms went up. The bout was over.

## From a Rohtak Village to an Asian Throne

Hooda was born on April 15, 2000, in Rurki village in Rohtak, Haryana — a district that has sent an unusual number of wrestlers and combat athletes to the national stage. She picked up boxing in 2011 at the age of eleven, training at a local academy before making her way into the national setup.

The progression was steady and then suddenly rapid. In 2022, Hooda won a gold medal at the Asian Boxing Championships and a bronze at the Women's World Boxing Championships — both in the same calendar year. That double planted her firmly in India's Olympic planning. Hangzhou 2023 brought a bronze, which stung because she had been among the podium favourites; Aichi 2026 brought the correction.

Her win on October 2 was part of a remarkable day for India at these Games: the country collected six gold medals in a single session, one of its best single-day hauls at any Asian Games. Hooda's gold also confirmed an Olympic quota place for women's 65 kg boxing for India at the Los Angeles 2028 Games — a result with consequences that extend well beyond the medal stand.

## Why This Matters

Women's boxing in India has been shaped by a small number of athletes who managed to sustain careers long enough to build a global record: Mary Kom is the name everyone knows; Nikhat Zareen and Lovlina Borgohain are the next generation. Hooda, at twenty-six, is now squarely in that company.

The Aichi gold does something specific: it secures her ranking, confirms her LA 2028 quota berth, and gives the women's boxing programme a proven 65 kg performer heading into the Olympic cycle. For a sport that operates largely out of public view between major Games, that kind of institutional certainty matters. India now goes to Los Angeles with a boxer who has won an Asian championship twice, medalled at Worlds, and answered the hardest question — what do you do when the first Games doesn't go to plan? — with a gold medal at the second.
