On 18 August 2026, the Ministry of Youth Affairs and Sports published the National Sports Awards 2025. No Khel Ratna was given — but the Dronacharya Award in the regular category went to **Chhote Lal Yadav**, a Subedar in the Indian Army who, over the better part of a decade, has quietly built one of the most remarkable coaching records in Indian women's boxing.

The names of his athletes say it better than any summary can. **MC Mary Kom**, five-time world champion and Olympic bronze medallist, had her career meaningfully extended under Yadav's guidance during what should have been her final years in the sport. **Jaismine Lamboria** won the World Boxing Championship title in Liverpool in 2025 and then converted that form into a **gold medal at the Commonwealth Games 2026** in Glasgow. **Sakshi Chaudhary** and **Arundhati Choudhary** also returned from Glasgow with gold medals, making Yadav's athletes responsible for three of India's historic seven-gold boxing haul at the Games.

## A Career Built on Two Setbacks

Yadav came to coaching through injury. As a boxer himself — a three-time national champion who competed at the sub-junior, junior, and senior levels — he had been part of India's national boxing setup from 2001 to 2012. When injury removed him from competition, he did not leave the sport. He turned to the coaching corner and, in 2014-15, completed a coaching diploma at the National Institute of Sports in Patiala, topping his batch. He then cleared the International Boxing Association's two-star coach certification in Amman, Jordan — one of a handful of Indian coaches to hold that qualification.

From 2017 to 2022 he worked as a coach within the national boxing camp, building the understanding of each boxer's technical needs and physical patterns that would later define his approach. The work on Mary Kom in those years was particularly visible: adapting training loads and ring strategy to allow an athlete past forty to compete at the highest level without breaking down.

In 2022, Yadav moved away from the central camp to run the Indian Army's women's boxing programme, with the support of Colonel Manoj Kumar and Colonel Mohan Roy. It was here that the next chapter of his coaching career took shape. Jaismine Lamboria, working under Yadav's direction, went from a Paris Olympics bronze medal contender to a world champion in Liverpool, and then to a Glasgow gold. Sakshi and Arundhati followed a similar arc — shaped in Army training environments into athletes who performed at the highest level when it mattered.

## The Quiet Side of the Award

The Dronacharya is specifically for coaches — it exists to name the people standing one step behind the spotlight. Yadav, by every account, has preferred that position. The athletes whose names appeared in medal ceremonies over the past five years rarely mentioned him first in post-victory interviews; the work happened in the gym, at the training board, and in individual conversations about what each boxer needed to win. The award, announced alongside 17 Arjuna Award recipients on August 18, is India's formal acknowledgment of what the results have shown steadily for years.

For Indian boxing — a discipline that has produced consistent results at the Commonwealth and Asian levels but struggled to translate that into Olympic medals — the Dronacharya going to someone working in the Army women's programme is a signal about where reliable excellence has been built. The next Commonwealth cycle, and the road to Los Angeles 2028, will tell us how far that programme can carry its athletes forward.
