Five days before his name appeared on an Arjuna Award list, Tejaswin Shankar stood on a podium in Glasgow holding a bronze medal that no Indian had held before. On August 13, he finished the Commonwealth Games 2026 decathlon with **7,976 points**, becoming the first Indian to win a medal in that event at the Commonwealth Games.

The Ministry of Youth Affairs and Sports announced the National Sports Awards 2025 on August 18. Shankar's Arjuna Award was notable partly because of its timing — arriving in the same month as the achievement it was recognising, which is not how these citations usually work.

## Ten Events, Two Days, One Podium

The decathlon places an athlete in ten disciplines across two consecutive days: 100m, long jump, shot put, high jump, and 400m on day one; 110m hurdles, discus, pole vault, javelin, and 1500m on day two. Winning requires no single exceptional event — it requires ten adequate-to-excellent performances, each scored on a points table, with the cumulative total deciding the final standing.

Shankar's **7,976 points** put him behind Lindon Victor of Grenada (**8,096**) and Damian Warner of Canada (**8,036**), both seasoned multi-eventers at the world level. Third place at Glasgow is, by any measure, a result the event had not seen from an Indian before.

The added context: Shankar's personal best — set at the Federation Cup in Ranchi — stands at **8,057 points**. That total would have been good enough for silver in Glasgow. He did not reach it at the Games; he was managing a patellar tendinitis problem that had forced him to withdraw from the high jump final at the same Commonwealth Games earlier in the week. That withdrawal, from an event where he held a CWG bronze from Birmingham 2022, drew attention. What followed in the decathlon two days later drew more.

For Shankar, the Glasgow bronze was his second Commonwealth Games individual medal. The Birmingham high jump bronze in 2022 was the first. He is now the first Indian to win Commonwealth Games medals in two different individual events — a distinction that reflects the unusual range his training covers.

## The Longer Story

Shankar, 27, grew up in Delhi and took up the decathlon through a route that many Indian multi-event athletes follow: starting as a specialist in a single event — in his case, the high jump — and expanding outward as coaches identified the physical profile that suits the combined events. He trains out of the United States part of the year, where the infrastructure for decathlon-specific preparation is more developed.

The patellar tendinitis that interrupted his Glasgow week is a recurring concern for athletes who train at this volume across this many disciplines. Managing it while competing at a major Games, completing the decathlon, and finishing third — that is what the Arjuna Award citation is recognising.

India has produced sprinters, jumpers, and shot putters who have won individual CWG medals. The decathlon has been a blank. It is not a blank any more.
