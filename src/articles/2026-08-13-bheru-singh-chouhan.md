For more than fifty years, Bheru Singh Chouhan has been singing the verses of Kabir from Bajrangpura, a village about 25 kilometres from Indore in Madhya Pradesh's Malwa region. On 13 August 2026, at Vigyan Bhawan in New Delhi, President Droupadi Murmu conferred the Sangeet Natak Akademi Award on Chouhan — the national academy's recognition for a lifetime of keeping the Nirgun devotional folk tradition of Malwa alive.

The award, covering the years 2024 and 2025, is conferred in a combined ceremony this year alongside 115 performers drawn from music, dance, drama, puppetry and folk and tribal traditions across India. Among them, Chouhan stands as a practitioner whose career has run almost entirely outside the circuits of metropolitan recognition — village gatherings, temple courtyards, cultural festivals in district towns — and who has arrived at Vigyan Bhawan not because he sought national platforms but because the music found its audience regardless.

Chouhan is 64. He began singing in childhood, imitating his father's bhajans in the fields of Malwa, and was performing across Malwanchal by his teens. In the five decades since, he has given more than **6,000 performances**, both within India and internationally. His primary instruments are the **tambura** and the **kartal** — the drone lute and paired hand-cymbals that anchor the Nirgun bhajan's unhurried, meditative sound. His repertoire reaches into the verses of Sant Kabir, Gorakhnath and Dadu, but Kabir is at the centre.

## What Nirgun Means

Nirgun bhakti — devotion to the formless — is the strand of Indian spirituality that refuses images and temples, preferring an inward attention that Kabir expressed in plain, confrontational verse. Kabir wrote about the hypocrisy of priests and the cruelty of caste at the same moment he wrote about longing for the divine; his poetry holds those two things in the same line. Chouhan has carried both into his performances.

He has not treated the tradition as a museum piece. His concerts incorporate Kabir's critiques of superstition, addiction and gender inequality into the flow of the bhajans — the social argument embedded in the spiritual song. Over more than half a century of performances, that combination has given him audiences at village level who come not just to hear old music but to be spoken to directly.

In April 2025, President Murmu conferred the **Padma Shri** on Chouhan at Rashtrapati Bhavan. The Sangeet Natak Akademi Award today completes a moment of national recognition that Madhya Pradesh's folk performers rarely receive at this scale.

Chouhan's career is a record of what sustained attention to a single tradition, over a lifetime, can produce. The academy's recognition names that. For Malwa — a region whose musical culture is deep and largely unrecorded — it also makes a visible claim: what happens in the fields and courtyards of a district a hundred kilometres from the nearest metro has value that travels.
