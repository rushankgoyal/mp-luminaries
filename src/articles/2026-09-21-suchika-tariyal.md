On September 20, 2026, at the Aichi-Nagoya Asian Games, Suchika Tariyal stepped off the mat having done something no Indian athlete had ever done: won an MMA medal at an Asian Games. The women's traditional 60kg bronze was the first MMA medal India has taken at the continental multi-sport event — a result made more significant by the fact that mixed martial arts was making its Asian Games debut that weekend.

The win was not a surprise to those who have watched Tariyal closely. It was, instead, the logical endpoint of a specific kind of athletic journey: one that began in judo, passed through the national MMA circuit, and arrived at the Asian Games carrying medals from every tier of the sport below it.

## Eight Golds in Judo, Then a New Discipline

Tariyal grew up in Rohtak, Haryana, and spent the first phase of her sporting career as one of India's foremost judo practitioners. She is an eight-time national judo champion. She won gold at the 2019 Commonwealth Judo Championships and at the 2019 South Asian Games. The judo base — grip strength, throws, timing on the mat — gave her the technical platform she later brought to MMA.

The transition was deliberate. Tariyal trained through the Sports Authority of India centre in Bhopal, building on her judo foundation with the striking and mixed-grappling work that MMA requires. She won the inaugural MMA-SFI National Championship gold, and won it again the following year. In January and May 2026, she took bronze at the Asian MMA Championships on both occasions, each result confirming she belonged at the top of the continental field heading into the Asian Games. She also competed in kurash — a Central Asian grappling discipline — at the Hangzhou 2023 Asian Games, giving her experience of performing at that level before MMA's debut.

At Aichi-Nagoya, she beat Mongolia's Altanchimeg Baigalmaa 2-0 in the quarterfinal. The semifinal, against Singapore's Teo Hui Hoon, went to overtime; Tariyal lost by a narrow margin and was placed in the bronze-medal bout, which she won.

After the result was confirmed, she said: "Asian Games medal means more than my life."

The phrasing is blunt, but the context gives it weight. Tariyal has spoken publicly about facing harassment early in her athletic career — a period when continuing the sport was not guaranteed. The journey from that point to a historic continental medal has run through consistent programme work at SAI Bhopal, a structured transition from a single discipline to a hybrid one, and results at every level of competition that prepared her for the one that mattered most.

MMA's arrival at the Asian Games opens a new competitive pathway for Indian combat-sports athletes. That Tariyal is the athlete who placed India on that map first — not through a shock result, but through a career built methodically over fifteen years — is the detail that the bronze medal will carry.
