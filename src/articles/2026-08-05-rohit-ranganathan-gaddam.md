The Electrochemical Society of India's Young Scientist Award lands differently when you know what it is rewarding. Dr. Rohit Ranganathan Gaddam, 35, runs the Clean Energy Research Group at IISER Bhopal's Department of Chemical Engineering. His group works on a specific and pressing problem: how to build batteries that are not dependent on lithium — a metal whose supply is geographically concentrated, increasingly expensive, and strategically contested. The 2026 ECSI award, announced this July, recognises the body of work his group has produced since he joined IISER Bhopal, and the momentum it is now building.

## Sodium, Not Lithium

Gaddam's primary research focus is sodium-ion batteries. Sodium is among the most abundant elements on Earth; unlike lithium, it does not depend on a handful of producing countries. A viable sodium-ion battery at scale would reshape the economics of energy storage for electric vehicles, grid backup systems, and consumer electronics. The engineering challenge is that sodium ions are larger than lithium ions, which changes how electrode materials absorb and release them and how quickly they degrade over charge cycles.

Gaddam's doctorate from the University of Queensland in Australia — funded by the International Postgraduate Research Scholarship and the UQ Centennial Scholarship — examined alkali-ion battery design at the materials level. He returned to India as a Humboldt Foundation fellow, a German government-backed fellowship awarded to researchers with demonstrable scientific records, before joining IISER Bhopal as a faculty member.

His group's work spans sodium-ion, lithium-ion, and solid-state battery architectures, with a focus on the cathode materials that determine how much energy a battery holds and how long it lasts. In March 2026, the group published research applying the Leidenfrost effect — a physical phenomenon more familiar from water droplets hovering over a hot pan — to the synthesis of cathode materials, producing electrode structures with better electrochemical performance. The approach is the kind of lateral thinking that characterises productive materials science: a well-understood physical effect, applied to a manufacturing challenge where it had not previously been tried.

In April 2026, Gaddam's first doctoral student, Sharad Pinjari, successfully defended a thesis on atomic-level doping in polyanion cathodes for sodium-ion storage — a milestone that signals the lab's graduation from a new group to one producing independent research output at a graduate level.

## Why Bhopal, and Why It Matters

IISER Bhopal is one of seven Institutes of Science Education and Research established by the Government of India to build research capacity at the national level. Its presence in MP matters: it recruits students from across the country and retains faculty who bring international research experience back to central India. Gaddam's career arc — Australian PhD, German fellowship, national-institute faculty position — is exactly the pipeline IISER was designed to support.

The ECSI Young Scientist Award places him among the recognised leaders of the electrochemical research community in India. For a country that has made battery technology a policy priority — from the national programme on advanced chemistry cells to EV adoption targets — building researchers with this depth at institutions outside the metropolitan research clusters is part of the longer answer.
