For 77 years, the people of Sidhi district watched India's railways expand all around them and never through them. That changed on 24 October 2026, when Union Railway Minister Ashwini Vaishnaw flagged off the first train from Churhat station—a milestone that residents of this coal-rich, forest-covered district in Madhya Pradesh's Vindhya region had been waiting for since before the country itself was born.

The inauguration brought two new rail sections into service: the Satna–Devendra Nagar line at **53 kilometres** and the Rewa–Churhat line at **65 kilometres**, together adding **118 kilometres** of new track to the national network. Both sections are part of the Lalitpur–Singrauli rail corridor, a long-planned project intended to bind the Vindhya plateau's scattered towns and industrial pockets into a single rail spine. Madhya Pradesh Deputy Chief Minister Rajendra Shukla joined the ceremony at Churhat station. Vaishnaw participated via video conferencing. A new **Devendra Nagar–Churhat MEMU service** was flagged off the same day, with regular passenger services opening to the public from 25 October.

## A Tunnel Through the Hills and a 77-Year Gap Closed

The engineering behind today's milestone was not simple. The Rewa–Churhat section alone required boring a **3.33-kilometre tunnel** through the Vindhya hills—exacting civil work in difficult terrain, years in the making.

Sidhi is a district of substance: it sits atop significant coal reserves, limestone quarries, and dense forest. But substance without connectivity does not easily become prosperity. Without a rail link, goods cost more to move, investment was slow to arrive, and residents relied on long road journeys to reach Rewa or Satna—the nearest rail towns—for every long-distance trip. The absence of a railway was not an administrative oversight but a compounded consequence of geography, terrain costs, and competing priorities. For decades, the project waited.

> "New rail links will boost connectivity and economic activity in the Vindhya region," Chief Minister Mohan Yadav said in a statement, adding that the lines would open new opportunities for industry, trade, and the people of Sidhi and the wider region.

The Lalitpur–Singrauli corridor, of which today's two sections are a part, spans hundreds of kilometres across Madhya Pradesh and Uttar Pradesh. It is designed to carry both passengers and freight, with coal and mineral traffic expected to be significant. Today's inaugurated sections knit Sidhi into that web for the first time.

**What changes now** is tangible. Rail access lowers transportation costs for local industries, draws attention from investors who previously bypassed road-only districts, and—for ordinary residents—replaces a long car or bus ride with a train ticket. The Vindhya region's natural resources and its people now have a direct path out.

Vaishnaw's inauguration of these 118 kilometres closes a gap that outlasted dozens of governments, railway budgets, and planning cycles. For Sidhi, the wait is over.
