Dindori is one of Madhya Pradesh's most tribal districts — a forested, hilly stretch of the Satpura range where health outreach has always required going further than a primary health centre. Reaching adolescent girls with two doses of an HPV vaccine, in a district where Banchhada communities, nomadic groups, and school dropouts often fall off every health register, is work that doesn't happen through passive administration. It requires a collector who is tracking it personally.

Anju Pawan Bhadoria, who has been the district collector of Dindori since September 2025, built the kind of active oversight that the programme needed. She held regular reviews at the collectorate, monitored coverage data, ensured coordination between health teams and schools, and pushed for outreach to populations that tend to be missed in aggregate numbers: nomadic tribes, Banchhadas, children in urban slums, and adolescent girls who had dropped out before school-based delivery could reach them.

The result placed Dindori in **Madhya Pradesh's top five districts for HPV vaccination** — a notable outcome for a district that rarely leads state health rankings. The achievement earned Dindori a **state-level award presented by Chief Minister Dr. Mohan Yadav and Deputy Chief Minister Rajendra Shukla**.

## The Team Behind the Numbers

District officials who received the award on behalf of Dindori were Chief Medical and Health Officer **Dr. Manoj Pandey**, District Immunisation Officer **Dr. R.K. Dogre**, and Auxiliary Nurse Midwife **Neem Kali** — the front-line team that translated Bhadoria's oversight into actual immunisation sessions in Dindori's villages and hamlets.

This is the structure that health programmes depend on: a collector who treats vaccination coverage as a personal accountability, health officers who translate that pressure into field deployment, and ANMs and ASHAs who physically reach the households. In Dindori's terrain, with its scattered population and poor road connectivity, the last mile is genuinely difficult.

HPV vaccination protects adolescent girls from the strains of the human papillomavirus that cause most cervical cancers. Cervical cancer kills more women in India each year than almost any other cancer, and the disease is largely preventable with early vaccination. The Central Government's programme aims to vaccinate girls aged 9–14 across India — but coverage gaps are largest precisely in remote tribal districts like Dindori, where girls also face the highest barriers to healthcare later in life.

Bhadoria comes to the role with an M.A. in Economics and an MBA in Human Resources. Her previous posting was as CEO, Zila Panchayat, Raisen. That administrative background — attuned to monitoring, coordination, and building accountable teams — proved well-suited to the kind of delivery challenge that Dindori's HPV programme required. The top-five placement is not an accident of geography; it is the outcome of ground-level priority.
