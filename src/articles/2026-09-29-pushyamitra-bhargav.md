On 1 April 2026, India's revised Solid Waste Management Rules came into force. They codified mandatory door-to-door collection, source segregation of wet and dry waste, and accountability for bulk generators — practices that Indore had been running at scale for nearly a decade. Mayor Pushyamitra Bhargav, who has led the Indore Municipal Corporation since January 2022, had been direct about where those rules came from: the city he runs was the template.

Indore's eighth consecutive **Swachh Survekshan** cleanest-city title, announced in 2026, arrived in the same year the national rules caught up to what the city had already proved possible. For the country, it was a policy update. For Indore, it was a confirmation.

Bhargav, 40, was elected mayor by a margin of **1.3 lakh votes** — the largest in the city's municipal history — and has since made the IMC's sustainability credentials a core part of his administration. Three years in, the figures are substantial. The corporation operates **100 garbage collection vehicles and 400 city buses** running entirely on compressed natural gas produced from the city's own organic waste. A **60-megawatt solar green bond** — India's first at the municipal level — was arranged to fund the city's renewable energy transition. During his tenure, **1.5 lakh saplings** were planted in a single day, and the city has developed **100 urban forests**.

## The Model Goes National

The mechanics of Indore's cleanliness are not mysterious. They are: daily door-to-door collection from every household, mandatory wet-dry-dry separation at source, a processing facility that converts organic waste into CNG at commercial scale, and consistent enforcement. The system has **2 lakh daily commuters** using the CNG bus fleet, meaning the waste-to-energy loop is visible to a significant portion of the city's residents every day.

What the SWM Rules 2026 add is national mandate. Cities across India are now legally required to implement door-to-door collection and source segregation — the same requirements that Indore made standard practice years earlier. Bhargav's argument to the Union government, made in the run-up to the new rules, was straightforward: Indore had demonstrated that these outcomes were achievable in a Tier-1 Indian city, at scale, with the resources available to a municipal corporation. The new rules formalised that demonstration as national policy.

On 6 September 2026, while sanitation workers took a rest day, Bhargav was among the municipal leaders who joined the **Hum Bhi Swachhagrahi** drive — picking up brooms themselves on the city's main roads to ensure uninterrupted cleanliness. The gesture was read by residents as characteristic: a mayor who shows up to sweep when the sweepers are off.

Indore has for years been the most-cited example in Indian urban governance of what sustained, specific municipal effort can accomplish. The SWM Rules 2026 are, in part, a formal acknowledgement of that — a city's practice becoming the country's standard.
