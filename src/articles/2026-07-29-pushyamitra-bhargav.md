When the Government of India notified its new Solid Waste Management Rules in 2026, replacing a decade-old framework that had failed to keep pace with the country's urbanisation, it looked at what worked. The four-stream waste segregation system that the rules now mandate nationally — separating wet, dry, sanitary, and special care waste at source — was already the standard in one Indian city. That city was Indore.

Pushyamitra Bhargav is the 24th Mayor of Indore. He took office in 2022, inheriting a city that had already built an unusual reputation: India's cleanest, for five consecutive years running. His predecessor had started something. Bhargav's task was to make it permanent and to push it further.

Under his leadership, Indore has extended that record to **eight consecutive years** as India's top-ranked city in the Swachh Survekshan. More importantly, the practices his administration has refined — door-to-door waste collection reaching nearly every household in the city, garbage vehicles running on CNG converted from the city's own organic waste, ward-level accountability systems — have now moved beyond Indore to become national policy. The city's CNG waste conversion programme currently powers **more than 100 garbage vehicles** and **400 public buses**, using fuel generated from what citizens discard.

## From One City's System to Every City's Law

The SWM Rules 2026, which took effect from April 1, require cities across India to adopt four-stream segregation at source, mandate digital tracking of waste movement through a centralised portal, and set source-level accountability standards. Nearly all of it reflects what Bhargav's administration has been running at scale in Indore for years.

The international attention has been striking too. An Australian delegation visited Indore to study how the city manages solid waste end to end — examining its collection systems, waste processing infrastructure, and community participation model. At home, Bhargav recently led a capacity-building workshop that brought together more than **250 elected representatives** from all 55 urban local bodies across Madhya Pradesh, sharing Indore's systems with smaller cities that are only beginning to build them.

Bhargav came to politics through law. Before his election to the mayor's office, he had served as the youngest Additional Advocate General of the Madhya Pradesh High Court. He has brought that precision to civic governance — treating solid waste not merely as a sanitation problem but as a systems challenge, with data loops, ward-by-ward monitoring, and feedback from citizens built into the process.

India has roughly 4,000 urban local bodies. Most of them are still constructing the basics that Indore has been refining for nearly a decade. As those cities now begin to implement the SWM Rules 2026, they will be drawing from a blueprint first built in Indore under Mayor Pushyamitra Bhargav. That is an unusual kind of influence for a city mayor to hold — invisible to most people, but lasting in its reach.
