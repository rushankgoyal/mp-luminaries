On September 24, amid a running debate in Madhya Pradesh about the future of state-funded medical education, Deputy Chief Minister Rajendra Shukla drew a clear line. Medical colleges built and funded by the state government, he said, would be operated and maintained by the state government — and that would not change.

"Medical colleges constructed by the state government or those being constructed with state government funds will be operated and maintained by the state government," Shukla said, directly addressing proposals that some colleges be handed to private operators under a public-private partnership model. "Why should the people of Damoh bear the burden of exorbitant fees and costly medical treatment?"

The statement carried weight because Shukla holds the Health and Medical Education portfolio in the Madhya Pradesh cabinet alongside his responsibilities as Deputy Chief Minister — he is the person who decides how the state's healthcare infrastructure is built and run. His ruling-out of PPP arrangements for state colleges preserves the fee structures and admission quotas that make government medical education accessible to students from rural and lower-income families in a state where private college fees can reach several times the state rate.

Shukla has represented the Rewa constituency in the Madhya Pradesh Legislative Assembly for multiple terms. He has served as Deputy Chief Minister since December 2023 and has used the health portfolio to pursue a steady expansion of medical infrastructure in a state where large parts of the rural interior — including the Vindhya region he comes from — have historically had thin access to specialist care.

## Building a Bigger Network

The PPP declaration came alongside a broader set of healthcare targets. MP currently has **19 government medical colleges**; Shukla's stated goal is to expand that to **26**, adding new capacity in districts that currently have none. A parallel programme is upgrading **348 Community Health Centres** across the state to First Referral Units — facilities equipped for obstetric care, emergency surgery, blood transfusion and specialist consultation, which is a significant step up from the basic services a CHC typically provides.

Incentive packages to encourage doctors and specialists to take up postings in remote areas are part of the same push. Healthcare in MP's forest-belt and tribal districts has long suffered from a staffing gap; the incentive framework is an attempt to make rural postings financially comparable to urban ones.

Taken together, the targets amount to a structural bet that expanding public supply is more durable than outsourcing it. A government medical college in a district produces trained doctors who often go on to serve in that district; it also anchors referral networks for smaller facilities around it. Shukla's September 24 statement ensures the colleges currently under construction will carry that logic forward rather than being transferred to operators whose primary obligation is to shareholders rather than patients.

For the **Vindhya region**, where Rewa sits, the combination of more medical colleges, upgraded Community Health Centres and incentivised rural postings represents the most significant structural investment in healthcare access in a generation.
