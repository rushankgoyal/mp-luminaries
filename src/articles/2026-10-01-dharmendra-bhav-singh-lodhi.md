On the morning of World Tourism Day — September 27, 2026 — Dharmendra Bhav Singh Lodhi stood before temple spires and stone pavilions at one of Madhya Pradesh's heritage sites and announced that every monument and museum in the state would throw open its gates for free. The gesture was symbolic, but the numbers behind it were anything but.

In three years, Madhya Pradesh's tourist arrivals have nearly doubled. In 2023, **11.19 crore visitors** came to the state. By 2024 the figure climbed to **13.39 crore**. In 2025 it crossed **21.03 crore** — a near-doubling in twenty-four months that few Indian states can match.

Lodhi, the two-term MLA from Jabera in Damoh district, took over the Tourism, Culture, and Religious Trusts portfolio when the Mohan Yadav government formed in December 2023. He inherited a state already gifted with raw material — Khajuraho's temples, Kanha's tigers, the ghats of Omkareshwar — but one that had historically punched below its weight on promotion and infrastructure.

## Ujjain Leads, and a Strategy Takes Shape

Within that 21-crore headline sits a more specific story: Ujjain alone drew **8.14 crore visitors** in 2025. The ancient city on the Kshipra River, home to the Mahakaleshwar Jyotirlinga and the sweeping Mahakal Lok corridor, has emerged as the single biggest draw in the state's tourism calendar. The corridor — an Rs 856-crore project that opened in phases — channelled pilgrim footfall into a planned precinct, easing congestion while adding facilities that kept visitors longer.

Lodhi's department did not build that corridor, but it has worked to replicate the underlying logic elsewhere: convert a pilgrimage stopover into a multi-day stay. His office has pushed for improved homestay networks around Chitrakoot and Orchha, negotiated direct rail connectivity to Panna for tiger-safari tourists, and expanded the Madhya Pradesh Tourism Development Corporation's budget for heritage-site restoration.

On World Tourism Day, Lodhi framed the goal plainly: "The Madhya Pradesh Government is firmly committed to the cultural resurgence of the state and to positioning Madhya Pradesh as one of the finest tourism destinations in India and on the global map."

That is the kind of sentence politicians say routinely. What gives it weight this year is the graph behind it — double-digit growth in arrivals for three consecutive years, a trend that predates the Yadav government but has accelerated under it.

## Why This Matters Beyond the Numbers

Tourism is often treated as a soft portfolio — useful for ribbon cuttings, easy to defund in a budget squeeze. In MP, though, it now directly touches the economy of districts that have few other growth engines. Damoh, Panna, Rewa, Orchha — these are not industrial hubs. But when a tiger-safari circuit in Panna fills its lodges, or when the Mahakal Lok draws pilgrims who also shop in Ujjain's old market, that money circulates through economies that need it.

A minister from Jabera — a town most Indians cannot place on a map — overseeing a portfolio that moved 21 crore people through his state last year is a quiet kind of success. The free-entry announcement on September 27 was a single day's gesture, but the trajectory it sits inside is the real story.
