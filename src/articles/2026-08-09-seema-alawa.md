On 15 August, when Independence Day medal citations are read at police formations across Madhya Pradesh, one name on the state's list of 21 honoured personnel will be that of Dr. Seema Alawa, Additional Deputy Commissioner of Police at Indore. The Ministry of Home Affairs selected her for the **Police Medal for Meritorious Service**, an annual recognition given on Independence Day to officers whose careers show consistent distinction.

Alawa has been in the Madhya Pradesh Police and Civil Services since **1998**, spending nearly three decades across postings in districts and police headquarters. In Indore, where she has served as Additional DCP, her work has included the implementation of India's integrated criminal justice system — when the city's fingerprint unit ranked among the best in the state for ICJS performance, it was Alawa who received the shield on behalf of Indore Police.

The work that may define her public career outside the force runs across two lines: academic research and tribal art.

## A Doctorate and a World Record

Her doctoral thesis, completed at Barkatullah University in Bhopal, examined what she titled "Challenges of Police Personnel at Workplace: A Gender Perspective" — one of the few systematic academic analyses of how gender affects careers within India's police institutions. The study looked at workplace rights for women, harassment, institutional barriers to leadership, and what it takes to make a police organisation genuinely inclusive. The thesis was expanded into a published book, **"The Role of Gender in Policing: Challenges, Rights, and Workplace Dynamics,"** launched at Indore's Pritamlal Dua Auditorium and aimed at police professionals, researchers, and policymakers.

The art started with a case.

In **2015**, serving as Additional Superintendent of Police in Jhabua — the tribal district in western Madhya Pradesh where the Bhilala and Rathwa communities have maintained the ritual painting form called Pithora for generations — Alawa was investigating a case when she encountered a Pithora painting on a village wall. The form stopped her: intricate narrative panels in vivid pigments, animals, village scenes, and cosmological figures, made as prayer. She began to learn it.

What grew from that encounter eventually became a single canvas **nearly 50 metres (164 feet) long**, hand-painted in the Pithora tradition and telling the entire arc of the Ramayana. The International Excellence Awards Book of World Records recognised this as the world's longest hand-painted artwork on a single roll of canvas.

Madhya Pradesh holds several of India's most distinctive tribal art traditions — Gond, Bhil, and Pithora among them — but formal state institutions rarely carry these forms to national attention. In Alawa's canvas, a living tradition from Jhabua's villages is carried outward, painted by a police officer who first encountered it on duty. The Independence Day medal arrives this month as a formal acknowledgement of what has been, by any measure, an uncommon public career.
