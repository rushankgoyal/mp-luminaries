On the morning of 22 September 2026, delegates from across India and more than thirty countries gathered at Bharat Mandapam in New Delhi for the 9th India International Water Week. Between keynote addresses by ministers and global water experts, the inaugural session paused to honour a group of community organisers called Water Warriors. One of them was Ram Kumari, who had travelled from Chhindwara district in Madhya Pradesh. She had not come as a policymaker or a researcher. She had come because she helped **225 families** in her community get clean drinking water.

Ram Kumari's work in Chhindwara district grew out of a simple problem that many rural households in central India still face: water that exists on paper — in government coverage maps and Jal Jeevan Mission records — but does not reliably flow to the tap. Pipes break, handpumps fail, water points are shared among too many families, and when access slips, the families who suffer most are the ones who were already marginalised. Women typically bear the brunt, walking longer distances and spending more hours fetching water.

Over the past several years, Ram Kumari has worked to close that gap in her part of Chhindwara. She built women's groups in villages across her area, convened regular meetings, and mobilised community members to report gaps in water access to local authorities and to Jal Jeevan Mission field teams. She tracked which households still lacked reliable drinking water, followed up on repair requests, and guided families through the process of registering complaints and accessing entitlements under government schemes. The combination of sustained pressure and grassroots accountability eventually produced a result that could be counted: around **225 families** now have access to safe drinking water who did not before.

## What the India International Water Week Recognises

The India International Water Week is the Ministry of Jal Shakti's flagship annual event, bringing together engineers, water scientists, international agencies, civil society groups, and government officials to discuss water management challenges and policy. The 2026 edition, running from 22 to 26 September at Bharat Mandapam, New Delhi, is organised around the theme "Climate Resilient Water Management." It was inaugurated by Vice-President C.P. Radhakrishnan in the presence of Union Minister for Jal Shakti C.R. Patil.

At the inaugural session, the Ministry specifically recognised grassroots Water Warriors — people whose contribution to India's water goals happens not at the policy level but at the household and village level, where the actual experience of water access or scarcity plays out. Ram Kumari was among those honoured in this category.

In a conference room otherwise filled with researchers and administrators, her recognition carries a clear message: the targets set under Jal Jeevan Mission — which aimed to provide tap water connections to every rural household in India — are only as good as the last-mile community work that makes those connections stay functional and trusted. That work is done by people like Ram Kumari, one village at a time.

For Madhya Pradesh, where Chhindwara district's forested and tribal areas have historically had lower water access coverage, community organisers who build trust and sustain pressure on local systems are not a supplement to official programmes — they are central to whether those programmes deliver on their promises.
