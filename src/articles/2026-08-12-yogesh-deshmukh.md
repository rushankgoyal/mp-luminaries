On August 15, India's President will present the President's Police Medal for Distinguished Service to Yogesh Deshmukh, the Additional Director General of Police heading Madhya Pradesh's Special Police Establishment. It is the highest honour India confers on a serving police officer for sustained excellence — and it recognises a career that has taken Deshmukh through some of the most exacting posts in the state's force.

Deshmukh joined the Indian Police Service from the 1995 batch, entering Madhya Pradesh Police in September 1996. Over the next three decades he worked his way through a range of assignments that rarely attract headlines but keep the state's governance machinery honest. He served as Additional Director General of Intelligence, overseeing the analysis and information networks that inform policing decisions across sixty-five districts. From there he moved to his current role: ADG of the Special Police Establishment, the Lokayukta's investigative wing, which probes corruption cases involving public servants.

Both postings demand patience rather than spectacle. Intelligence work means managing sources, verifying information, and briefing decision-makers — often with no public credit. Lokayukta work means building watertight case files against officials who frequently have institutional and legal resources of their own. Neither role fits neatly into a headline, which is precisely why the President's Medal matters: it acknowledges the value of unglamorous, long-term service.

## Thirty Years of Demanding Postings

The President's Police Medal for Distinguished Service is not a single-event reward. The Ministry of Home Affairs awards it to officers who have served at least twenty-five years with exceptional distinction — combining professional conduct, operational effectiveness, and a record free of serious disciplinary findings. Deshmukh's citation covers a career spanning the late 1990s through the mid-2020s, a period that saw Madhya Pradesh Police modernise its investigation practices, expand community policing and — through the Lokayukta SPE — handle a rising caseload of anti-corruption inquiries.

This Independence Day, **four Madhya Pradesh police officers** will receive the President's Distinguished Service Medal, and **seventeen more personnel** will be honoured with the Medal for Meritorious Service — a total of 21 from the state at a national tally of 982 awardees drawn from police forces, fire services, home guards and correctional services across India.

For Madhya Pradesh Police — a force of roughly 85,000 officers — the number of honours reflects a consistent culture of recognition that reaches from the DGP's office all the way down to constable-level personnel being honoured for decades of field service.

For Deshmukh specifically, the medal arrives at the close of a career arc that took him from entry-level posting through district commands and intelligence desks, and finally to the top of the state's anti-corruption investigative apparatus. The President's Medal is an acknowledgement that those decades of demanding, publicly invisible work made a material difference to how Madhya Pradesh is governed.

When police officers in difficult, thankless roles are recognised at the national level, it also signals to the force itself that sustained excellence — not just dramatic incidents — is what the system values. That signal, repeated each Republic Day and Independence Day, is one of the quieter but real ways in which institutions reinforce professional standards.

Madhya Pradesh, with its large geographic spread and the complexity of administering 230 assembly constituencies, depends on that kind of institutional health. Yogesh Deshmukh has contributed to it for thirty years.
