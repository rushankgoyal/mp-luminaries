Glasgow, Scotland. 31 July 2026. Harsh Singh stepped onto the tatami for the men's -60kg judo final at the Commonwealth Games and walked off as a gold medallist. He defeated Australia's Joshua Katz **10-0 by Waza-Ari** in a bout that was never close. In doing so, the 23-year-old Havildar from the Indian Army made history that Indian judo had been chasing across every edition of the Games: he became the **first Indian male judoka to win a Commonwealth Games gold medal**.

The same evening, Asmita Dey had given India its first-ever women's judo gold at a Commonwealth Games, completing a historic golden double. But the men's side of Indian judo had never stood on the top step of a CWG podium — until now.

## A Flawless Path Through Glasgow

Harsh Singh's route to the final was as commanding as the final itself. In the round of 16, he opened with an Ippon victory over Malawi's Chikondi Kathewera. In the quarterfinals, he again won by Ippon, this time against Vanuatu's Alan Monthouel. His semifinal against Australia's Pedro Carlos Antun Neto was decided by a Waza-Ari — the same margin by which he closed out the gold-medal match against Katz. Through four bouts, he conceded nothing.

The performance reflected preparation that has been building across major IJF events over the past two years. Harsh competed at the 2025 Tokyo Grand Slam, the 2025 Hong Kong Asian Open, the 2026 Senior Asian Championships, and the 2026 Tashkent Grand Slam before arriving in Glasgow. This Commonwealth Games was his first major multi-sport competition, and he won it on his debut.

Born on 24 May 2003, Harsh Singh grew up in a judo household. Both his parents are former judokas, and his father has coached at the national level, including as Australia's national judo coach at previous Olympic Games. That lineage gave him technical foundations unusual in Indian judo. He now trains at the **Inspire Institute of Sport (IIS)** in Bellary, Karnataka — a centre that has produced multiple Indian champions across combat and field sports.

His rank, Havildar, places him in the non-commissioned officer tier of the Indian Army. Army sports programmes have long been the backbone of India's individual sports pipeline, providing athletes with the stability, coaching support, and international exposure that club structures alone rarely can. Harsh Singh's gold is, in part, a product of that system performing exactly as designed.

The result matters beyond the medal count. India's women's judo contingent had already signalled the country's rising depth at the Games — Asmita Dey's gold and Yamini Mourya's silver confirmed the pattern. A men's gold, won in a dominant manner by a 23-year-old in his first major Games, adds a new dimension to that picture. It gives the national programme a men's benchmark it did not have before, and it gives coaches, selectors, and young judokas a name to point to when someone asks whether Indian men can compete at the top of the Commonwealth Games podium.

The answer, as of 31 July 2026, is yes.
