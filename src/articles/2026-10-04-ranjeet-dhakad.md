Most of the people who keep India's tigers alive do not have names that appear in the news. They work before dawn, patrol terrain that offers no shelter, and deal with emergencies — a snared animal, a flood, a poaching attempt — with equipment that a city office would consider inadequate. Ranjeet Dhakad is one of those people. On September 10, the work that has defined his career was formally acknowledged for the first time.

Dhakad, a forest guard at Madhav Tiger Reserve in Shivpuri district, was presented the inaugural Machhli Award at a ceremony in Bhopal. The award — named after Machhli, the celebrated tigress of Ranthambore who became one of the most photographed wild animals in history — was instituted jointly by the Prabha Khaitan Foundation and WWF India to recognise frontline wildlife conservation workers. The ceremony was presided over by Ramniwas Rawat, Chairman of the Madhya Pradesh Forest Development Corporation.

## What the Award Recognises

The Machhli Award is not given for a single act. The citation covers the accumulation of years: regular patrol duties in areas of the reserve that see low visitor footfall and high human-animal interface, systematic tiger monitoring that feeds into the reserve's population data, wildlife rescue operations, and emergency response during flood seasons when the reserve's terrain becomes dangerous and animals occasionally wander into adjoining villages.

Madhav Tiger Reserve was designated India's 58th tiger reserve in March 2025, bringing formal Project Tiger status and increased resources to what had long been an under-resourced landscape in central MP. The reserve sits in the Vindhyan hill range, stretching across roughly 375 sq km of dry deciduous forest. Its tiger count has grown steadily since protective measures were strengthened, and the daily work of guards like Dhakad is central to that trend.

Dhakad was not the only recipient: Naveen Chandra Mishra from Kanha Tiger Reserve jointly received the award in the field staff category, and Range Assistant Ranjana Nagar won the women's category. The Vanya Prani Mitra Award — a companion recognition for community contributions to wildlife conservation — was presented to a forest-adjacent community group that has collaborated with the reserve on human-wildlife conflict mitigation.

## Why It Matters for MP's Wildlife Infrastructure

Madhya Pradesh has long anchored India's tiger conservation story. Kanha, Bandhavgarh, Pench, and now Madhav — the state holds six tiger reserves, more than any other state in the country by number. The national tiger census has consistently placed MP at or near the top of the all-India count. That record does not emerge from policy alone; it is built by the staff who work the reserves daily.

The Machhli Award fills a gap that has been noted by conservationists for years: frontline workers receive training and nominal compensation, but rarely public recognition. An institutionalised award — backed by WWF India, a credible independent body — changes that calculus slightly. It names individuals, creates a record, and signals to the next generation of forest staff that the work is seen.

For Ranjeet Dhakad, the recognition arrives after years of work in a reserve that only recently achieved tiger-reserve status. His name is now in the public record of Indian wildlife conservation — which, given the obscurity in which most of this work is done, is not a small thing.
