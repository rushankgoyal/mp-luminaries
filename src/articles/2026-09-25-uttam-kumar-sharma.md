On the morning of **18 September 2026**, a cheetah designated KGP12 gave birth to four cubs inside Kuno National Park in Sheopur district, Madhya Pradesh. KGP12 is herself India-born — one of the second generation to arrive in this country not from Namibia or South Africa, but from within Kuno's boundaries. The birth brought India's total cheetah count to **56**, and it arrived one day after the park's most significant anniversary: **17 September 2026** marked exactly four years since Prime Minister Narendra Modi released the first eight Namibian cheetahs at Kuno in a ceremony that launched Project Cheetah.

The Field Director who oversees day-to-day operations at Kuno is **Uttam Kumar Sharma**, a 1999-batch Indian Forest Service officer from Madhya Pradesh cadre. Managing Kuno through its fourth year has meant navigating the full arc of a reintroduction programme — the deaths, the political scrutiny, the veterinary interventions, the expansion of the population, and now the emergence of India-born animals producing cubs of their own. At the 2025 Eco Warrior Awards, Sharma was recognised in the category for "Innovative Initiatives," a citation attributed to his operational work on Project Cheetah.

## The Logic of a Second Generation

KGP12's birth matters beyond the headline number. A reintroduction programme is not a transfer of animals — it is the attempt to establish a self-sustaining wild population. The critical test is whether animals born in captivity or in another country can survive, adapt, and reproduce in a new landscape. When their offspring then reproduce, that cycle has turned a second time. KGP12 and her four cubs represent exactly that: a cheetah born in India, surviving to adulthood at Kuno, and now raising cubs in the same park.

India's cheetah count stood at around 24 when the Namibian cohort arrived in September 2022. The figure of 56 in September 2026 reflects births across multiple litters and the subsequent Kuno-to-Gandhisagar relocations that have expanded the programme's footprint, though losses to disease and injury have also occurred. The net trajectory over four years is upward, and the ratio of India-born animals in the population has increased with each generation.

Kuno National Park covers approximately 750 square kilometres of grassland and dry deciduous forest in the Chambal region of western Madhya Pradesh. Before the cheetahs arrived, it had been cleared of human habitation in a process that took decades and displaced several thousand people from the Saharia community — a history that Project Cheetah has always carried alongside its conservation goals. The park's current work under Sharma's direction includes monitoring, veterinary care, habitat management, and the logistics of a programme that involves the Ministry of Environment, the Wildlife Institute of India, and international partners.

For Madhya Pradesh's forest service, the fourth anniversary of the cheetah's return to India is a point of stock-taking. The animal had been absent from the subcontinent since the 1950s. Getting it to a count of 56 — with India-born animals now producing their own young — is the kind of result that field administrators rarely see in a single posting.
