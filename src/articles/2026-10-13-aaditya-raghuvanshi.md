At the 65th National Open Athletics Championships in New Delhi, Aaditya Raghuvanshi cleared **2.16 metres** in the men's high jump final to win bronze — a height that, at the 5th National Open Jumps Competition in Bengaluru in March 2026, had been enough to win the event outright. That the same clearance now earns only bronze says something about the field he was competing in. It also says something about how far Raghuvanshi has come.

The Bhopal-based high jumper trains at the Madhya Pradesh Athletics Academy. His route to the national open podium has been methodical: junior meet records, then inter-state appearances, then a breakthrough at the open level in early 2026 where he finished with 2.13 metres at the Bengaluru competition, just behind Commonwealth Games-level competitors. Seven months later, he stood on the podium at the country's most open-age senior championship with a clearance three centimetres higher.

Before the open-level results, Raghuvanshi established himself in junior competition. At the MILO National Inter-District Athletics Meet, he set the event's high jump record — a mark that brought him to the attention of the Athletics Federation of India's selectors and alongside him in preview coverage was only Shaili Singh, one of India's highest-ranked female long jumpers. At the National Junior Athletics Championships in Guntur, he cleared **1.95 metres**, at the time a performance that placed him ahead of the under-16 field and within range of the senior national standard.

## The arc of a technical athlete

High jump is one of the more technically demanding events in athletics. The Fosbury Flop — the arched, back-first technique used by all competitive jumpers since the 1968 Mexico City Olympics — requires a precisely timed curved run-up, a specific take-off foot, and a back-arch at the bar that takes years to ingrain. Getting the approach angle wrong by a foot, or mistiming the jump by a fraction of a second, brings the bar down regardless of strength or speed. Athletes who come to the event early and stay in it long enough to master the technique tend to keep improving well into their mid-twenties.

Raghuvanshi's progression supports that pattern: the NIDJAM record as a junior, then 1.95m in competition, then 2.13m in March 2026, then 2.16m at the national open in October. The curve is steady, and at 2.16m he has now reached the height where India's best jumpers compete.

The 65th National Open ran from October 8 to 11 at Jawaharlal Nehru Stadium in New Delhi, drawing athletes from across the country's state programmes and services teams. Madhya Pradesh athletes won four medals in total — two gold and two bronze. Raghuvanshi's bronze in the high jump joined Arjun Waskale's 1500m gold, Kuldeep Kumar's pole vault gold from the first day, and Vinod Singh's 10,000m bronze in the state's haul.

For the Madhya Pradesh Athletics Academy, the combination of a pole vault gold, a 1500m gold, a 10,000m bronze and now a high jump bronze at a single national open championship reflects the breadth of the programme's current strength. For Raghuvanshi specifically, clearing 2.16 metres on this stage, with this field, sets a new benchmark from which his next season begins.
