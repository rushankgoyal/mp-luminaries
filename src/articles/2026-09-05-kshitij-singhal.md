On September 7, Union Environment Minister Bhupender Yadav will hand Indore the **Clean Air Appreciation Award** at a ceremony in New Delhi — a national honour that puts Madhya Pradesh's commercial capital at the front of India's fight against urban air pollution. The award comes from the Swachh Vayu Survekshan 2026, the Ministry of Environment's annual ranking of **130 cities** on how rigorously they act on their National Clean Air Programme city action plans. Indore has now earned the recognition in back-to-back years.

The IAS officer driving this outcome is **Kshitij Singhal** (2014 batch), who took charge as Indore Municipal Commissioner in January 2026. He arrived with a reputation for exactness and a tolerance close to zero for paperwork that does not translate into field results. In the nine months since, Indore's air-quality programme has run at a pace that neighbouring cities study with envy.

## What Indore did differently

The initiatives Singhal oversaw read less like a government checklist and more like a systematic assault on particulate matter at every source. Road-cleaning machines replaced manual broom sweeping on primary roads, reducing the dust clouds that follow early-morning traffic. **300-plus active construction sites** were covered with netting and subjected to daily water-sprinkling, cutting the fine-dust dispersal that typically spikes between October and March. Green belts expanded across arterial roads and flyover margins, adding a living barrier between traffic exhaust and residential areas. Waste from demolition and construction — a chronic source of uncontrolled dust — was brought under systematic collection and processing.

Singhal also pushed the public-facing side of air quality. Neighbourhood notice boards now carry AQI readings, and a campaign to explain the link between burning crop residue, construction dust, and respiratory health reached communities that had never previously engaged with the idea of "air quality" as a civic responsibility.

The results are visible in Indore's NCAP monitoring data, which tracks particulate levels across the city. Indore's Swachh Vayu Survekshan 2025 ranking — first in its population category, which covers cities above ten lakh — was the benchmark Singhal inherited and extended through 2026.

Jabalpur also won a Clean Air Appreciation Award this year, and Bhopal's Ward No. 60 took the first-ever micro-ward recognition. Madhya Pradesh is the only state with three simultaneous national clean-air honours in 2026.

## Why it matters

India has 130 cities under the National Clean Air Programme with a mandate to cut particulate pollution by **40 per cent by 2026**. Most of them are struggling. Indore is not. It is the city that other city commissioners are asked to visit and report back on — the model that the NCAP framework's architects point to when explaining that the targets are, in fact, achievable.

For Singhal, the September 7 ceremony is not a destination. He has made clear to his team that the survey ranking is a floor, not a ceiling, and that the city's air monitoring infrastructure needs to grow faster than the traffic it is measuring. Indore has long known how to win cleanliness rankings. It is now learning — under this particular commissioner — how to win clean air ones too.
