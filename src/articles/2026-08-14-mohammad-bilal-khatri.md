When dignitaries sit down for the 'At Home' reception at Rashtrapati Bhavan on 15 August — the President's traditional high-tea gathering on Independence Day — the round table covers beneath their cups will carry a pattern born in the river valleys of western Madhya Pradesh.

They will be in Bagh Print: the centuries-old block-printing tradition from the town of Bagh in Dhar district, distinguished by hand-carved wooden blocks and dyes drawn from natural, vegetable-based sources. The craftsman responsible for these pieces is **Mohammad Bilal Khatri**, a national award-winning artisan who has spent his life in the workshops of Bagh, carrying forward a technique his family has practised across generations.

Khatri worked on the commission alongside his brothers Kazim Khatri and Abdul Karim Khatri. The project was developed in collaboration with the National Institute of Design (NID), Ahmedabad, which designed the ceremonial invitation kit commissioned by Rashtrapati Bhavan. The kit draws on traditional crafts from Chhattisgarh, Goa, Madhya Pradesh and Maharashtra. Bagh Print represents the Madhya Pradesh contribution — appearing both on the round table covers for the reception and on the cover page of the official invitation cards dispatched to distinguished guests.

## A Craft That Reads Like a History of the Land

The designs Bilal Khatri chose for the presidential commission draw on layers of identity that Bagh Print has carried across centuries. Ancient cave and floral motifs echo the carved frescoes of the Bagh Caves — Buddhist rock-cut monuments dating to the fifth and sixth centuries, standing a few kilometres from the town itself. Sacred marigold patterns appear alongside rare ancestral tribal textile patterns: the Juwariya, Janjiri-Dhari and Attha motifs historically worn by the indigenous communities of the Dhar region.

This depth of reference is part of what distinguishes Khatri's work. He has brought sustained study to a craft that lesser practitioners reduce to repetition. The Government of India has recognised him with the National Merit Handicraft Award, the Vishwakarma Award and, in 2021, a National Handicraft Award for his contribution to evolving stamp printing within the Bagh Print tradition.

## Why This Matters for Madhya Pradesh

Handicraft traditions in India carry a persistent risk of erosion — younger generations in craft towns pulled toward other livelihoods, markets that reward speed over skill, documentation that lags behind what practitioners actually know. Bagh Print has survived that pressure, in part because craftsmen like Khatri have stayed in the workshops rather than leaving.

The presidential commission is a different kind of recognition: not just an award in a list, but a decision, at the level of India's highest civic ceremony, to let the work represent the country. On the 80th Independence Day, when President Droupadi Murmu hosts dignitaries at Rashtrapati Bhavan, the cloth on the table will have been made in a small town in western Madhya Pradesh, by hands that learned the art in that same town.

For Dhar district — and for the broader ecosystem of artisans and block-printers across MP — it is the kind of placement that no advertisement can replicate.
