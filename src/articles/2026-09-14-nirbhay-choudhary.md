At the Jawaharlal Nehru Stadium in New Delhi on September 11, Nirbhay Choudhary launched his first throw at the Indian Athletics Final Series — and did not need a second. The **71.02 metres** his hammer covered was further than any Indian has ever thrown one, erasing a national record that had stood for ten years and making Choudhary, at 21, the first from his country to clear the 71-metre mark.

The previous mark — **70.73m** — had belonged to Neeraj Kumar since 2016, a ceiling that had held through years of expansion in Indian athletics: new academies, expanded coaching programmes, back-to-back Commonwealth and Asian Games campaigns. Choudhary did not come through any of that infrastructure. He trained at the stadium in Delhi where his father, Naresh Choudhary, a former hammer thrower himself, had once competed. He studied the mechanics of elite international throwers not through coached sessions but through whatever footage he could access online. He learned the event, in significant part, from YouTube.

## A Record With an Awkward Footnote

The margin between Choudhary and the rest of the field on September 11 was difficult to ignore. Damneet Singh threw **68.12m** for second place — more than two and a half metres behind. Ashish Jakhar was third at 67.51m. In a competition the Athletics Federation of India had structured as the final domestic tuning event before the Asian Games, the man who topped the field by the widest margin was not the one going to Aichi-Nagoya.

Damneet Singh, who will represent India in the men's hammer throw at the Asian Games beginning September 19, was selected before this meet. Selection had been finalised; Choudhary's record came just after the window closed. He will watch from home while the athlete he outthrew by more than two metres competes for India in Japan. It is a situation that raises a straightforward question about how the Athletics Federation of India calibrates its selection criteria — one that will presumably need an answer before the next major squad is named.

The fact of the record, however, stands clear of the footnote. Choudhary threw the hammer further than any Indian before him. The previous holder of that distinction had set his mark during the Rio Olympics year; the event had not moved beyond it in the decade since, despite everything that changed in Indian track and field during that period. It moved on September 11.

His father's presence in the background gives the story something a record number alone does not supply: a sense of how long this preparation actually took. Naresh Choudhary did not break national records in his own career. He threw the hammer at a time when the sport offered very little infrastructure or attention in India. That his son has now gone further than any Indian hammer thrower in history — guided partly by technique passed down within the family and partly by video clips of champions on a phone screen — is a story that the national athletics programme will have to reckon with, in whatever way it chooses, over the next season.

Whether the federation corrects the selection anomaly or treats September 11 as a standalone result, Nirbhay Choudhary's position in the record book is not provisional. He is the best hammer thrower India has produced.
