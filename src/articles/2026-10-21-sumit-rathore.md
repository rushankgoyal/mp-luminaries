Dalauda is a small town in Mandsaur district, where the Chambal plateau meets the edge of central Madhya Pradesh. It has a market street, a handful of restaurants, a pharmacy — the kind of town where everything you need is within a kilometre, but a family sharing one two-wheeler cannot always get there and back without effort.

Sumit Rathore grew up in Dalauda. He left to build a career in technology, but the logistics problem of his hometown stayed with him. When he, his brother **Vaibhav Rathore** and co-founder **Ankit Agrawal** decided to build a solution, they began in 2025, in Dalauda itself, under the name BelivMart. On **21 October 2026**, the company — relaunched as **Furtilo** in January 2026 — announced it had raised a seed round from Trikona LLC, a US-based early-stage investment firm.

## The model and the reach

Furtilo's approach starts with the merchant side of the problem. In a small town, a restaurant or a kirana store has thin margins. Joining a large delivery platform at 25–30% commission per order can cut deeper than the profit itself. Furtilo instead charges a **flat monthly subscription**: merchants pay once, riders deliver, customers order via the app. No per-order cut.

The company reports it is live in **37-plus towns** across Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan and Gujarat — Dalauda among them — with more than **650 merchants**, **70-plus delivery partners** and **6,000-plus monthly orders** as of this month (all figures are company-reported). Funding will go toward hiring, two new service lines — Parcel Delivery and Bike Taxi — and an expansion to **500-plus small towns by mid-2028**.

> "The shops were nearby, but there was no easy way to get things home," Sumit Rathore, Furtilo's co-founder and CTO, said of the idea's origin.

## Mandsaur district to 500 towns

India's large aggregator apps have scaled deep into metros and Tier-2 cities, but most of the country's small towns remain outside their footprints. The order density and average ticket size in a Dalauda or a similar small town do not justify the customer-acquisition spend that works in Pune or Indore.

Furtilo's founders are betting that a ground-level, subscription-first model built by people who grew up in exactly these towns can crack the economics. The decision to start in Dalauda rather than in Bengaluru — the company relocated there only after the model held — is the clearest evidence of that conviction.

For Madhya Pradesh's Mandsaur district, where agriculture and small retail drive the economy, a startup that makes it easier for local shops to reach local customers is not an abstract tech story. It is a practical improvement to the daily economy of towns that larger platforms have passed over.
