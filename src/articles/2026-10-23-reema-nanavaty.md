On the evening of October 15, in Georgetown University's Gaston Hall in Washington, D.C., Reema Nanavaty walked on stage to receive the Hillary Rodham Clinton Award from the former United States Secretary of State herself. The honour, given by Georgetown's Institute for Women, Peace and Security, recognises individuals who have advanced women's rights, economic empowerment, and the cause of peace. Nanavaty, the general secretary of the Self-Employed Women's Association of India, shares the distinction this year with Nobel laureate Muhammad Yunus and entrepreneur and philanthropist Tory Burch.

For anyone who knows SEWA's story, the award lands with weight. The organisation Nanavaty has led since 1999 is India's largest union of informal-sector workers — **3.2 million members** across more than half the country's states, all of them women, almost all earning a living from work that formal economies still struggle to count: street vending, domestic labour, agricultural piece-work, home-based craft, and construction. SEWA is their union, their bank, and often their only insurance company.

## Forty Years of Unglamorous Work

Nanavaty joined SEWA in 1984 as a young programme worker. She learned every corner of the organisation before being elected general secretary in 1999. The Government of India awarded her the Padma Shri in 2013 for her contributions to social work.

The numbers that mark her tenure are large and specific. SEWA's food security programme, expanded under her leadership, now covers **one million households**, providing nutrition support and market linkages to women farmers and their families. The Hariyali initiative she oversees connects rural members to solar energy, cutting fuel costs and giving women a stake in India's renewable energy transition. After the Gujarat earthquake of 2001 and later disasters, SEWA's rehabilitation teams — trained under her watch — helped women rebuild their livelihoods without waiting for outside relief. Similar work has reached communities in Afghanistan and Sri Lanka.

Georgetown's award citation frames this work through a precise lens: economic security for women is not a welfare outcome, it is a peace outcome. Communities where women hold income, assets, and agency are demonstrably more stable. SEWA's model — workers organising themselves, setting their own terms, and building their own financial institutions — has been studied and adapted by organisations across South Asia and sub-Saharan Africa.

The recognition at Gaston Hall is a long way from Ahmedabad's Ellis Bridge market, where Ela Bhatt founded SEWA in 1972 with a group of women selling used cloth. For India, Nanavaty's award is a reminder that some of the country's most significant social infrastructure was built without fanfare — one membership card at a time — and that the women who built it have something to teach the world about what peace can look like.
