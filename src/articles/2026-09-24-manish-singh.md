When Madhya Pradesh launches the Mukhyamantri Sugam Parivahan Bus Seva on 25 September 2026, connecting hundreds of cities, towns and inter-state corridors under a single ticketing and tracking system, the launch will be the visible result of roughly two years of invisible work. That work was led by Manish Singh, a 2009-batch IAS officer who serves as Secretary of the Transport Department and Managing Director of Madhya Pradesh Yatri Parivahan and Infrastructure Limited (MPYPIL) — the state company set up specifically to build this network.

Singh's team started at the beginning. They conducted field surveys in all **55 districts** of the state before designing a single route. The surveys mapped where people actually travel — not just the corridors assumed to be high-demand, but the smaller inter-district connections that existing private operators had neglected. From that ground-level data, the team designed **150 city routes**, **450 intercity routes** and **436 interstate routes** — more than 1,000 routes in total, structured to ensure that no habitation above a threshold is outside the network's reach.

The technology layer received equal attention. Every bus in the system is fitted with GPS tracking, CCTV cameras, and a panic button for passenger safety — managed through an Integrated Traffic Management System (ITMS) that lets the state monitor fleet position in real time. On the last-mile problem, Singh's team signed an MoU with Rapido, the bike-taxi and auto platform, to bridge the gap between bus stops and final destinations — an acknowledgement that a good trunk network fails passengers if the first and last kilometre remains unaddressed.

## Building the Conversation First

In September 2026, Singh chaired the Yaatri Parivahan Vision Summit in Indore — a two-day convening on 11–12 September that brought together transport officials, private operators, urban planners and technology vendors. The summit was not a launch event but a working session: it surfaced implementation problems, resolved coordination gaps between city-level and state-level operations, and produced a shared operating framework for the network's first phase.

Hosting it in Indore, the state's commercial capital and a city with one of India's more active transport discussions, reflected a deliberate choice. Indore had already been experimenting with BRT and shared mobility solutions; the Sugam Parivahan network would anchor its city routes there first.

Manish Singh also serves as CEO of the Inter-State Transport Authority — the regulatory body that governs cross-border bus permits — giving him jurisdiction over both the state and interstate dimensions of the network. That alignment of authority mattered: interstate services require permits from multiple state governments, and having a single officer hold the relevant regulatory mandate alongside the operational MD role reduced the number of inter-departmental loops that would otherwise slow approvals.

For travellers in Madhya Pradesh, the practical promise is a bus service where the schedule is published, the vehicle is trackable, and the route is designed around where passengers live rather than where operators find it easiest to run. For a landlocked state whose road connectivity has long outpaced its public transport infrastructure, that shift is overdue.
