The first thing Shailendra Pratap Singh did when he arrived at Government Middle School Kanjwar was count the students. There were 69. In a rural corner of Sidhi district — one of Madhya Pradesh's remote Vindhyan hill tracts — that number was not a starting point. It was a warning.

On 5 September 2026, Teachers' Day, President Droupadi Murmu presented Singh with the National Award to Teachers at a ceremony in New Delhi. He was one of six Madhya Pradesh educators among 82 recipients chosen from across the country — and one of the few in the group whose work has played out entirely in an underserved rural public school, not a city institution.

Sidhi is a challenging posting. The district is hilly, partly forested, and home to large tribal and economically marginalised communities where families face competing pressures to keep children — particularly girls — at home or at work rather than in school. Child marriage persists. Access to education for disabled children is patchy at best.

## How 69 Became 202

Singh approached the problem as an organiser first, a teacher second. He went door to door. He spoke to parents, community leaders and panchayat members. He built the case that school had practical value — that literacy was not an abstract goal but a route to better options for their children.

The numbers that followed tell the story. Enrolment rose from 69 to **202 students**. Daily attendance climbed from **44 per cent to 96 per cent** — a shift that requires not just persuasion on the first day of school but sustained trust-building across months and years. Dropouts were eliminated. Child marriages that had been keeping girls out of school were prevented through ongoing community engagement.

For students with severe disabilities, Singh arranged daily transport — a concrete intervention that removed one of the most direct barriers to attendance for families who could not carry or walk a child to school each morning.

On the infrastructure side, he was equally persistent. He mobilised **over ₹5 lakh** in community contributions, secured **0.17 hectares** of additional land for the school, and obtained government sanctions worth **₹76 lakh** for new building construction. A school that had been running in inadequate facilities now had the beginnings of a proper campus — funded partly through the community it served.

The National Teacher Award is given to 82 teachers annually across India, selected for impact on students, on institutions, and on the educational fabric around them. In 2026, Madhya Pradesh's six recipients came from different disciplines and settings. Singh's profile — a government school teacher working in a remote district without the resources or visibility that urban schools attract — represents the award in its most direct form: recognising what a single teacher, working persistently in a hard place, can actually accomplish.

Madhya Pradesh's rural school network faces long-standing challenges — low enrolment, high dropout rates, and the steady pull of agricultural and domestic work that competes with classroom attendance. Teachers who manage to reverse those trends through sustained individual effort are doing something that matters far beyond their own classrooms. In Sidhi's Kanjwar village, Shailendra Pratap Singh spent years doing exactly that. On Teachers' Day 2026, the President of India noticed.
