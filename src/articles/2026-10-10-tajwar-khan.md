The gold and silver threads running through a batua purse from Bhopal's old city don't look like a policy statement. But when Tajwar Khan accepted first prize in the Best Art and Craft Exhibition at the 7th Madhya Pradesh Travel Mart on October 9, 2026, she carried with her the history of a craft that has outlasted empires, survived the end of royal patronage, and found new relevance in a city that is still learning how to value what it makes by hand.

Khan's label — Zee by Tajwar — competes in a space where the work is measured in millimetres: the tension of the metallic coil against the fabric, the angle of each hand-placed bead, the weight and shimmer of finished embroidery laid flat. At the Travel Mart, held at the Kushabhau Thakre International Convention Centre in Bhopal, her stall took the top award in the art and craft category, ahead of Reena Bhuria's Wood Craft entry in the runner-up position. The award was presented on the final day of a four-day event that drew delegates from 34 countries and recorded over **10,000 business-to-business meetings**.

## A Craft That Survived Centuries

Zari Zardozi is Persian in origin — "Zar" means gold, "dozi" means embroidery — and it arrived in India through Mughal patronage, reaching its peak under Emperor Akbar in the 16th century. When later rulers withdrew their support, artisans dispersed across the subcontinent, and Bhopal became one of the new centres for this tradition. The Begums of Bhopal — four successive women rulers who governed the princely state for nearly a century — gave the craft a powerful second life, educating workers and building a dedicated market for intricate gold-thread work.

Today the tradition survives in the narrow alleys of Bhopal's old city. Artisans sit cross-legged on the floor, bent over an adda — a wooden frame that holds the fabric taut — and use a hooked needle to pull metallic coils and beads through in tight, precisely spaced formations. The signature product remains the **batua**: a small embroidered clutch purse that features at weddings, family ceremonies, and formal occasions. One experienced Bhopal artisan has trained hundreds of women in the technique; the craft has been a source of income for women in the old city for generations.

The Madhya Pradesh government recognised the craft's significance by naming it Bhopal's product under the **One District One Product (ODOP) scheme**, a national programme designed to protect and promote district-specific traditional industries. The designation brings visibility, market access, and training support — tools that help artisans and labels like Zee by Tajwar reach buyers beyond the locality.

Khan's work takes those traditional skills and connects them to a contemporary design sensibility. Her label applies the Zardozi vocabulary to garments, accessories, and jewellery-adjacent pieces, finding new formats for the metallic-thread tradition. The MPTM award puts that work in front of international buyers and tourism operators who may not have known Bhopal's embroidery heritage before walking past her stall.

The Travel Mart is deliberately designed to do exactly that — to move craft, culture, and heritage from local knowledge to global recognition. The 7th edition signed **12 strategic MoUs** and attracted **₹1,100 crore in investment proposals** across hospitality, wildlife tourism, and heritage development. Within that larger canvas, Tajwar Khan's first-prize win is a reminder that the most lasting work is often done by hand, on a wooden frame, in an old city alley, one stitch at a time.
