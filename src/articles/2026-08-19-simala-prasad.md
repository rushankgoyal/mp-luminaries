On 15 August 2026, as India marked its Independence Day, Simala Prasad stood among a small cohort of Madhya Pradesh police officers recognised with the Meritorious Service Medal — a national decoration for exceptional service over a sustained career. The honour arrived as her name was becoming familiar to a second audience entirely: cinema audiences.

Prasad is a 2011-batch IPS officer of the Madhya Pradesh cadre, currently posted as Deputy Inspector General of Police (Women's Safety) in Bhopal. She cleared UPSC in her first attempt, finishing **51st all-India** — a number that announces itself without embellishment. Those credentials are the expected part of her profile. What is less expected is that in June 2026, she appeared on screen as the lead in *The Narmada Story*, a Hindi feature film set in Narmadapuram district — the stretch of central Madhya Pradesh through which the Narmada river runs and from which the city takes its name.

## A Story Set in Her Own State

In *The Narmada Story*, Prasad plays Sub-Inspector Narmada Raikwar, a police officer working a case in the river's heartland. It is the kind of casting that arrives as completion rather than novelty: a working police officer, in the uniform she wears to the office, playing a police officer on screen in the state she has spent fifteen years serving.

The film's ensemble reaches well beyond the novel premise of its lead. Anjali Patil, a National Award-winning actress, appears alongside Zarina Wahab and Ashwini Kalsekar — names that give *The Narmada Story* a seriousness of artistic intent that the film deserves to be judged on.

This is not Prasad's first appearance in front of a camera. Her debut came with *Alif* in 2016. *Nakkash* followed in 2019, alongside Kumud Mishra and Sharib Hashmi — actors with substantial careers in Hindi cinema. She has also published writing: policing, cinema and authorship have coexisted in her professional life without apparent contradiction, and without either crowding out the other.

Her father served as a Member of Parliament, which means public life runs in the family. Whether that background shaped her decision to join the IPS at 22, or her willingness to engage with the arts alongside it, Prasad has let the body of work speak rather than the biography.

## Why It Matters

Madhya Pradesh's Women's Safety division exists because violence against women in the state requires a dedicated, coordinated response — specialised units, helplines, protocols. Prasad heads that work from Bhopal while simultaneously producing art rooted in the geography she administers.

*The Narmada Story* is also a small piece of something that matters at the state level: a modest but growing number of Hindi films that take rural Madhya Pradesh — not Mumbai, not a metropolis — as their subject and their landscape. A DIG who can run a policing division and put a river district on screen is a specific kind of storyteller, and Madhya Pradesh has one in her.
