In a hall filled with decorated educators from across India on 5 September 2026, Reena Gupta was honoured for a body of work that rarely makes headlines: **16 years** of teaching computer and digital skills to hearing- and visually-impaired students at a government vocational institute in Ujjain.

President Droupadi Murmu presented Gupta with the National Teachers' Award 2026 on Teachers' Day, placing her among 48 educators selected from across the country for their innovation and lasting impact in education. She was one of **six teachers from Madhya Pradesh** to receive the award — a strong showing for the state in a national competition that drew candidates from every corner of India.

## The workstation, not the classroom

Reena Gupta's teaching life is built around computers and patience. As a training officer at Government Divisional ITI (Industrial Training Institute) in Ujjain, her students are not the typical college-bound youth pictured in most education coverage. They are young people with hearing and visual impairments who have come to the ITI to learn a trade — specifically, the digital skills that open doors to employment in today's economy.

Her course load covers computer operation, digital applications and the navigational skills that make a workstation usable for someone who cannot rely fully on sight or sound. This is specialist instruction, and Gupta has been doing it for a decade and a half without the kind of institutional recognition that comes more easily to teachers in better-resourced settings.

The National Teachers' Award citation describes the outcome of her work in two words: **employable** and **self-reliant**. Those two words carry weight. Students who come to Government Divisional ITI Ujjain with a disability leave with a qualification and, in many cases, a job. That is not a given anywhere in India; in MP's smaller cities, where formal employment opportunities for persons with disabilities can be scarce, it reflects the sustained effort of instructors like Gupta.

## Why the ITI matters

Government ITIs exist at the practical edge of India's education system. They train people who need a trade, not a degree, and they do it at a price most families in smaller towns and districts can afford. What they accomplish depends almost entirely on the people who teach in them.

For Madhya Pradesh, where government ITIs serve a large population across districts that have limited access to private technical institutes, the quality of instruction inside those buildings matters enormously. Gupta's 16 years at Ujjain's ITI represent exactly the kind of unglamorous, consistent, high-stakes work that the National Teachers' Award exists to find and name.

On 5 September, India's President did exactly that.
