Most students who say they hate mathematics have never been shown what it looks like to enjoy it. Sangeeta Yadav, who teaches mathematics at St Joseph's Convent Sr. Sec. School in Idgah Hills, Bhopal, decided years ago that this was a problem she could solve — classroom by classroom.

On 5 September 2026, Teachers' Day, President Droupadi Murmu presented Yadav with the National Teacher Award at Vigyan Bhawan in New Delhi. She was one of six educators from Madhya Pradesh to receive national recognition at the ceremony, and one of **82 teachers** from across India honoured for exceptional contributions to education.

The award, given by the Ministry of Education, recognises innovation in teaching, positive impact on students, and sustained dedication to the profession. In Yadav's case, the specific innovations are well documented.

## Five Programmes, One Goal

Yadav has designed five distinct learning programmes that run throughout her school year:

**GANITOTSAV** — a full mathematics festival — asks students to create their own games, explain concepts to peers, and compete in collaborative challenges. What it removes is the examination as the only context in which students relate to mathematics. What it adds is ownership.

**MathEMind** focuses on mathematical reasoning rather than computation. Students are asked not just to find an answer, but to explain why their approach works and where it would fail. The habit of thinking about thinking is the outcome.

**Mathletics** is the competition stream. Yadav has taken students from classroom exercises into district and state-level mathematics competitions, giving them a setting in which mathematical confidence has real stakes and real rewards.

**Quest for Mathematics** takes students outside the standard curriculum — into logic puzzles, recreational mathematics, and open-ended problems that have no single correct method.

**Bagless Mathematics Day** clears desks of textbooks and notebooks entirely. Students work with physical objects, manipulatives, and peer discussion to grasp abstract ideas by touching them.

Together, these five programmes rest on a consistent premise: that students who seem bad at mathematics are, more precisely, students who have not yet found their way into it.

## A Classroom That Proves the Point

Yadav's school operates in central Bhopal and serves students from a wide range of backgrounds. The activity-based approach she has built does not depend on expensive equipment or digital infrastructure — it depends on the teacher's design and on the belief that anxiety in a mathematics class is not a character trait but a problem with a solution.

Madhya Pradesh has been investing in its PM SHRI model schools and digital classrooms. What Yadav's national recognition underlines is a complementary truth: that pedagogical creativity — the kind funded by a teacher's effort rather than a capital budget — can produce equally documented results.

When President Murmu presented the award in New Delhi, she was recognising not only a classroom in Bhopal, but an argument that applies everywhere. Mathematics is not a subject that some students simply cannot learn. It is a subject that, when taught as a festival rather than a test, most students are happy to attend.
