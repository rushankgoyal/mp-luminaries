On 1 September 2026, Raja Babu Singh will step into the rank of Director General of Police — the highest substantive grade available to a state-cadre IPS officer in Madhya Pradesh. The announcement, which circulated in police circles this August, marks the end of a career arc that began in a farming family in Banda and has taken Singh through three decades of steady, structural reform inside one of India's largest police forces.

Singh joined the Indian Police Service in 1994. Over the course of his career, he earned both the Police Medal for Meritorious Service and the Police Medal for Gallantry — decorations that trace opposite ends of police work: institutional excellence and the decision made in a dangerous moment.

## The Khaki Revolution

The episode most discussed in connection with his elevation is what colleagues began calling the "Khaki Revolution" — a sustained overhaul of training culture across Madhya Pradesh's **eight police academies** while Singh served as Additional Director General (Training).

Before his tenure, police training in MP ran largely on drill and repetition: marching formations, rule memorisation, physical endurance. Singh added a different layer. He introduced interactive simulation exercises that place trainees inside reconstructed scenarios — a crowd-control situation, a domestic dispute, a first-responder callout — and require them to make decisions in real time. Alongside that came structured stress-management sessions and a real-time feedback loop in which trainers formally assess each exercise and trainees receive written evaluations rather than a verbal reprimand or a passing grade.

The scale of the effort: **more than 4,000 trainees** across the eight schools went through the new curriculum. The aim was not to replace physical training but to ensure that the constable or inspector who graduates is comfortable thinking under pressure, not only standing at attention.

Singh is currently posted as ADGP Railways in Bhopal, a role that places him at the intersection of policing and the infrastructure millions of Madhya Pradesh residents use daily. The elevation to DG rank takes effect next month.

## Why It Matters

Madhya Pradesh's police force covers a state that stretches from dense urban centres like Bhopal and Indore to remote forest districts where a constable may be the first and only government officer a village sees in a week. Reform at the training stage — before officers ever reach their first posting — is the structural lever that the force cannot buy its way around. It compounds.

A constable who learns to debrief and reflect in the academy carries that habit into the field. Multiply that across 4,000 officers, across eight districts, and the effect reaches communities Singh may never visit.

His is a story that Madhya Pradesh tends to produce quietly: a first-generation officer from a farming background who does not seek the camera, who modifies the institution from inside rather than from the top, and whose promotion arrives not as a political calculation but as a recognition of accumulated, verifiable work.
