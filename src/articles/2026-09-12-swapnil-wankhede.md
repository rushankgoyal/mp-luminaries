On September 7, a Sunday that marked the International Day of Clean Air for Blue Skies, Union Environment Minister Bhupender Yadav handed Swapnil Wankhede the Clean Air Appreciation Award at Indira Paryavaran Bhawan in New Delhi. The award was the country's formal acknowledgement that Jabalpur, under Wankhede's watch, had become one of the three cleanest million-plus cities in India.

The fifth Swachh Vayu Sarvekshan — the central government's annual ranking of cities on air-quality improvements — placed Lucknow first, Indore second, **Jabalpur third**, and Bhopal fourth. Three of India's four most improved large cities were from Madhya Pradesh. That result is the yield of a sustained, unglamorous campaign: fixing roads so dust does not rise, expanding parks and tree cover, running street-washing machines through commercial zones, managing solid waste at source, and replacing broken stretches of unpaved lanes with paver blocks. None of these projects make for a headline. Together they moved Jabalpur up a bracket in a national survey that measures outcomes, not intentions.

## A Commissioner Who Treats Pollution Metrics Like a Budget Line

Wankhede cleared the UPSC civil-services examination on his fourth attempt, placing at All India Rank 132 in the 2015 batch. That trajectory — four years of focused effort before cracking one of the country's hardest tests — maps onto the administrative approach he brought to Jabalpur. The city's clean-air programme he oversees is not a single project but a stack of neighbourhood-level interventions: pollution controls tied to construction sites and industrial clusters, community participation drives that pull residents into plantation campaigns, and infrastructure upgrades that remove friction between daily life and cleaner air.

Municipal commissioners in India often spend their tenures on fire-fighting — drainage complaints, property-tax collection, protest management. Wankhede has done that work too. But the Swachh Vayu Sarvekshan result points to something deliberate: a measurable improvement in the quality of the air Jabalpur's 30-odd lakh residents breathe, delivered through routine administration rather than any single dramatic intervention.

At the New Delhi ceremony, Municipal Commissioners from award-winning cities across the country were present. The presence of the Jabalpur, Indore, and Bhopal commissioners in the same recognition cohort signals that Madhya Pradesh has built something reproducible — a model of urban environmental governance that holds across the state's different city sizes and political settings.

For Jabalpur, which sits at the junction of freight routes and carries heavy traffic through its central neighbourhoods, a third-place finish in clean-air rankings is not an accident. It is the work of a commissioner who treats a spreadsheet of pollution metrics as seriously as a budget line.
