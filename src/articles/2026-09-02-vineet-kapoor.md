The 20th International Human Rights Summit ran for three days — July 30 to August 1, 2026 — inside United Nations Headquarters in New York. Representatives from 82 countries attended; the theme was "Education for Peace." Among the law enforcement leaders recognised at the summit was an IPS officer from Madhya Pradesh most people outside policing circles have never heard of.

Dr. Vineet Kapoor, currently posted as Deputy Inspector General (Community Policing) in the Madhya Pradesh Police, received the **Human Rights Hero Award** — an honour given to practitioners whose work demonstrably improves lives on the ground rather than in policy documents. For Kapoor, the ground is the slum colonies of MP's cities, and the work is a programme called **Srijan**.

## What Srijan actually does

Srijan — the word means "creation" in Hindi — is a community policing initiative Kapoor has built over several years that works directly with children and young women in low-income urban settlements. Instead of waiting for crime to happen and then responding to it, Srijan embeds police officers as coaches, mentors, and facilitators in communities that have historically viewed the khaki uniform with suspicion rather than trust.

The programme runs skill workshops, sports leagues, digital literacy sessions, and creative arts programmes in areas where children have few extracurricular options. Girls' safety and empowerment form a specific strand of the work. The results have attracted attention well beyond Madhya Pradesh: the International Association of Chiefs of Police (IACP) recognised Srijan with an award in Boston in 2024, and the programme has been cited by UNICEF, UN Women, the Supreme Court's Juvenile Justice Committee, and the Madhya Pradesh High Court's JJ Committee. In 2025, Kapoor presented Srijan's findings at the DG-IG Conference before the Prime Minister.

Kapoor brings unusual academic grounding to this work. He holds a Chevening Scholarship-funded MA in Human Rights from the University of Essex in the UK, an MA in Development Geography from the Delhi School of Economics, and a PhD in Human Rights and Organisational Development. He has also been nominated for a UN senior police adviser role (P5 level) with UNFICYP. The Human Rights Hero Award at the New York summit is, in a sense, the international peer validation of what has been building over years at the neighbourhood level in Madhya Pradesh.

## Why it matters for MP

Community policing has been a stated priority for police forces across India for decades, but implementation has been uneven — often reduced to PR exercises rather than structural engagement with vulnerable communities. What makes Srijan notable is the consistency of its institutional recognition across very different bodies: an American policing organisation, a UN agency focused on children, a UK university's human rights centre, and India's own judicial committees. That breadth of endorsement suggests the programme has cleared multiple independent verification bars.

For Madhya Pradesh, an officer doing this quality of work at the district and community level is an asset that rarely makes front pages. The Human Rights Hero Award from a UN-venue summit is a reminder that some of the state's most consequential public servants are not the ones with the highest public profiles — and that the work of rebuilding trust between citizens and institutions is slow, unglamorous, and worth celebrating precisely because it is.
