On the afternoon of 14 September, National Hindi Diwas, Chief Minister Mohan Yadav stepped into Bharat Bhavan — Bhopal's landmark multi-arts complex on the shores of the Upper Lake — and inaugurated something India rarely stages in one place: every mother tongue at once.

The Bharatiya Matrubhasha Anushthan, running September 14 and 15, drew senior journalists, litterateurs, and poets from across the country to a two-day national conclave on linguistic diversity. The theme — *Matribhasha, Sanskriti aur Bharatiya Jeevan-Moolyon Par Samvad* (Dialogue on Mother Tongue, Culture, and Indian Values) — set the frame: languages are not merely communication tools. They carry knowledge systems, oral traditions, and ways of understanding the world that disappear when a tongue fades.

## What Bhopal Brought Together

The event was organised by Veer Bharat Nyas and the Directorate of Culture, Government of Madhya Pradesh, in partnership with five institutions: Bharat Bhavan itself, the Makhanlal Chaturvedi National University of Journalism and Communication, the Deendayal Upadhyaya and Dattopant Thengadi Research Institute, and Matribhasha Manch.

Bharat Bhavan, designed by the architect Charles Correa and inaugurated in 1982, was a deliberate choice of venue. Correa built the complex specifically to host multiple art forms at once — theatre, poetry, visual art, and tribal arts share its tiered terraces and open-air pavilions. Hosting a conclave on linguistic plurality inside a structure designed for cultural plurality was pointed, not accidental.

National Hindi Diwas marks the date in 1949 when the Constituent Assembly adopted Hindi as India's official language. The Anushthan took a wider view: the title "Matrubhasha" means mother tongue, not Hindi specifically. By gathering writers and scholars in languages from across India, the event framed Hindi as one voice in a large, diverse chorus rather than a single national answer.

Madhya Pradesh sits at the crossroads of several language families. Hindi dialects, Bundeli, Bagheli, Malvi, Nimadi, Gondi, and Korku are all in daily use across the state's 55 districts. Any serious national conversation about protecting India's linguistic diversity passes through the state whether it chooses that role or not.

Since taking office in December 2023, Mohan Yadav has paired cultural programming with the state's economic agenda. The Vikramotsav festival ran for **139 days** across MP and beyond; investment summits have been framed around the state's civilisational identity; now a national language dialogue has been given Bharat Bhavan as its stage. The argument, made consistently, is that culture is not decoration — it is the foundation on which a state's identity and confidence rest.

For the scholars and poets arriving at Bharat Bhavan this September, the two days of conversation on mother tongues, community identity, and constitutional commitments to linguistic pluralism will feed into discussions on education policy, media in regional languages, and the place of India's hundreds of smaller languages in an era of digital homogenisation. Madhya Pradesh, on this occasion, chose to be the place that hosted those questions.
